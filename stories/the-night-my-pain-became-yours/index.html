The Night My Pain Became Yours

The market at Harrowgate smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and frying dough, and for four days I had loved every minute of it.

Crowds were safe. Crowds were a kind of hiding. In a crowd, nobody looks at your hands.

I kept mine buried in the pockets of my gray cloak as I drifted between the stalls, a seamstress's niece from the south, no one in particular. That was the story I told anyone who asked. It was the story I had told for most of my life. In this part of the world, a healer is a thing from legends, and legends are not loved. Legends are hunted.

I had a single copper in my pocket and a list of three things to buy: thyme, lamp oil, and a needle. Ordinary things. Ordinary things were all I wanted.

I was haggling over the thyme when the first pain arrived.

It did not feel like my pain. That was the strangest part. It came from nowhere, sharp and exact, a line of fire drawn along my left side, just beneath the ribs, as if someone had pressed a heated blade there and dragged it slowly down.

I gasped. The old woman behind the stall stopped mid-sentence.

"Girl? You've gone white."

"It's nothing," I said. "The heat."

It was not hot. It was a cold gray morning, and my breath smoked in front of my face.

I pressed my palm to my side. No wound. No blood. Not even a tear in the wool. And yet the burning went on, deeper now, and with it came a terrible certainty that I was not hurt at all. Something was hurting through me.

I took my thyme, I took my change, and I walked away with what I hoped looked like a steady stride.

Four steps. Five. On the sixth, the cold arrived.

I have healed fevers, broken bones, and a farmer's hand that a thresher had nearly taken off. I know what pain feels like when it passes from one body into mine, the way a healer must let it. It is a thing you open the door to. You choose it.

This was not that. This came through the wall.

The cold slid under my ribs and spread outward, slow and heavy, like river water filling a boot. Behind it came something worse than cold. Anger, but not mine. It tasted like iron. It was old and patient and it had teeth, and underneath it, so deep I almost missed it, there was a grief so tired that my knees nearly gave out.

I turned into the narrow alley behind the dyer's shop, where the stones were stained blue and red and the air stank of vinegar and lye. I caught the wall with both hands. The world swam.

Think, Lira. Think.

Four nights ago, in a ruined chapel at the edge of the forest, I had made a mistake.

One mistake. One desperate, stupid, merciful mistake. A man had been bleeding out on the cold floor, and I had done the thing I had sworn I would never do again. I had put both hands on a stranger and let the gift loose, all of it, without holding anything back.

I told myself that it was over when I walked away before dawn. I told myself he was a stranger, and that strangers do not follow you.

I remembered his eyes, though. Dark, furious, and so terribly alive, fixed on mine as the wound closed under my palms. I remembered that he had grabbed my wrist, and that when his fingers touched my skin, something had turned over inside me, like a key in a lock I had not known was there.

The pain in my side flared white. I bit down on my own sleeve so I would not scream.

And then, because I am what I am, because I have never in my life been able to hear suffering and do nothing, I did the only thing my body knew how to do.

I reached for it.

I did not mean to. I only wanted the burning to stop. I let my hands go warm against the wet stone, and I let the gift rise, the way it does, like a breath held too long and finally released.

It did not stay inside me.

I felt it leave. I felt it travel, thin and bright, along a thread I could not see, stretching away from me through the streets, over the rooftops, toward something far to the north. Something that pulled back.

And far away, someone drew a breath.

I heard it. I swear I heard it, as clearly as if he had been standing at my shoulder. A ragged, shuddering exhale, the sound of a man who has been carrying a weight so long he has forgotten it was a weight, and who has just, for one heartbeat, set it down.

The burning in my side dimmed.

The grief did not.

Then, without warning, a picture that was not mine unfolded behind my eyes. A long hall of black stone. Candles guttering in a draft that came from nowhere. A hand, scarred across the knuckles, resting on a table beside a goblet that had not been touched. And a woman's voice, low and frightened, saying a single word that I could not catch.

Then it was gone, and I was alone in a stinking alley with my cheek against cold stone and the taste of someone else's memory on my tongue, like ash.

I slid down the wall until I was sitting in the dye-stained mud, and I did not care.

A healer's gift is supposed to flow in one direction. Out. Every whispered story I had ever heard agreed on that much. You give, and you bleed a little, and you recover. Nobody had ever warned me that something could flow back.

Nobody had ever told me that the door could open from the other side.

I do not know how long I stood there. A cart rattled past the mouth of the alley. A child laughed. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, ordinary, indifferent, as though the world had not just tilted on its axis and slid sideways.

My hands were shaking. I looked at them, those hands that had gotten me into so much trouble, and I understood something I did not want to understand.

When I had healed him, I had thought I was giving something away.

I had not given. I had traded.

A shadow fell across the alley. I flinched, but it was only the dyer, a thick-armed woman with indigo to her elbows, frowning down at me.

"You're ill," she said. "Go home, child."

"I'm well," I lied. "Only dizzy."

"You're leaning on my wall."

"I'm leaving your wall."

She grunted, unconvinced, and went back to her vats. I straightened my cloak, tucked the thyme back into my pocket, and made myself walk out into the square, because the one thing a hunted woman must never do is look like she is running.

The market was exactly as I had left it. Fishmongers shouting. Lamplighters on their ladders. A juggler dropping one of his pins and cursing in three languages.

And yet something had changed. The pain was gone, but it had left behind an awareness, a strange, humming pressure at the very edge of my mind, like a second shadow cast by a second sun. I could feel direction. I could feel distance. I could feel that whatever I was tied to was not far away anymore.

It was moving. It was moving toward me.

My pulse hammered. The crowd shifted, and the sound of the square seemed to fall away, until all I could hear was the slow, heavy rhythm of a heart that was not mine, beating in time with my own.

I turned.

At the far end of the square, beneath the dripping arch of the old grain hall, a man was standing very still while the crowd flowed around him like water around a stone. Tall. Black coat, travel-stained. A hood pushed back from a face I had last seen white with blood and rage.

One of his hands was pressed to his left side, just beneath the ribs. Exactly where mine had been.

He was not looking at the crowd. He was not looking at the stalls, or the guards, or the lamplighters.

He was looking at me.

And in my chest, in a place I had never felt before, something that was not my heartbeat answered his.
